# How the reader actually reads a card

A running list of what the target reader — Delhi NCR, roughly 18 to 30, phone in hand — does and
does not take in. Every entry came from watching a card fail, not from theory.

These are **not** style preferences. Each one changed a score.

---

## 1. The sentence stops being read before it ends

**Observed 2026-09-10, on the merged `SCEN` card.**

> The place has a good aesthetic, and the design and architecture are the highlight.

The reader gets as far as *good aesthetic … design and architecture* and stops. The word
**highlight** is the entire test — it is what separates *this is the point of the night* from
*this is a nice thing* — and it arrives last, where it is not processed. The card gets read as a
list of positive nouns, agreed with, and scored high by people for whom `SCEN` is not remotely a
priority.

**Fix:** move the load-bearing word off the end, and make the claim personal.

> The place has a good aesthetic, and the design and architecture are the main highlight of the
> place **for you**.

*for you* forces the reader back into the sentence. It is doing the work a trailing *highlight*
could not.

**Rule: the word that makes a card discriminate must never be the last word.** If a card only
works when read to the end, it does not work.

---

## 2. A concrete noun becomes the whole card

**Observed 2026-09-10, on the merged `PLAY` card.**

> A table with a game already set up, and you are playing and keeping score.

*A table with a game* reads as a **board game**, and the rest of the sentence never widens it.
Someone who would happily spend a night at an arcade or a bowling alley scores this low, because
they answered the question *do you want a board game* — which is not what `PLAY` measures.

**Fix:** offer two images, not one.

> A table with a game already set up, **or you on your feet** playing games and keeping score.

**Rule: a factor with more than one physical form needs more than one image in the card.** The
first concrete noun anchors the whole card, so if that noun covers only part of the factor, the
card measures only that part.

---

## 3. A card that describes something good measures nothing

**Observed 2026-09-10, across `SCEN`, `FOOD`, `LIVE` and `NOV`.**

> A plate you would still be thinking about next week.

Nobody turns down a memorable meal. The card has to test whether the thing is *the reason you
left the house*, not whether it is nice — so it needs a comparison built in: *above anything
else*, *the spotlight of the room and not the people*. A card everyone agrees with sorts nobody.

**Rule: every card must give the people it is not for something to reject.**

---

## 4. Write in the vocabulary the reader already uses

**Observed 2026-09-10, on `SCEN`.**

*Good aesthetic* is not a description — it is the phrase this group reaches for unprompted. The
earlier wording, *the look of the place is the first thing that catches your eye*, is better
English and worse as a card, because it has to be translated before it can be answered, and a
card that gets translated gets reasoned with.

**Rule: use the reader's words, not the correct ones.** Applied narrowly — a set written entirely
in borrowed slang would date badly and read as pandering. This is a tool for the one or two cards
where a native phrase exists and is exact.

---

## 5. A question mark invites a puzzle

**Observed 2026-09-10, on `NOV`.**

> A place that opened last month and nobody has told you about yet.

*Nobody has told you about it* prompts **why not — is it bad?** The reader starts solving rather
than answering, and the card measures suspicion instead of appetite for somewhere new. Adding
*and you are still thinking of going* turns the unknown from a problem into the appeal.

**Rule: a card describes a scene the reader is standing in. The moment it poses a question about
that scene, they answer the question instead of the card.**

## Open
